Clustering Findings
1. Methodology
We implemented K-Means clustering on behavioral features extracted from hourly consumption data. 

Why not cluster on raw data?
  - Each household has ~8,760 hourly readings (365 days × 24 hours).
  - Clustering directly on this would be noisy and computationally expensive.
  - Instead, we extract "behavioral fingerprints" that summarize each household's patterns.

Features Used (4 total):

  1. avg_daily_consumption (Magnitude)
     - What: Average kWh consumed per day.
     - Why: Separates "big consumers" from "low consumers."
     - Example: A large family home (~25 kWh/day) vs. a single-person apartment (~5 kWh/day).

  2. winter_summer_ratio (Seasonality / Heating Proxy)
     - What: Ratio of average consumption in winter (Dec-Feb) vs. summer (Jun-Aug).
     - Why: Identifies households with ELECTRIC HEATING.
     - Example: Ratio > 2.0 means winter consumption is 2x summer → likely electric heating.
               Ratio ≈ 1.0 means stable year-round → likely gas heating or no heating load.

  3. night_ratio (Time-of-Day Behavior)
     - What: Percentage of daily consumption occurring at night (10pm - 6am).
     - Why: Identifies "Night Owl" households or those with night storage heaters / EV charging.
     - Example: Ratio > 30% → significant night usage (water heater, EV, shift workers).
               Ratio < 10% → mostly daytime usage (typical family).

  4. cv (Coefficient of Variation = std / mean)
     - What: Measures how "spiky" or irregular consumption is.
     - Why: Distinguishes steady baseload consumers from erratic users.
     - Example: Low CV (~0.3) → consistent daily routine.
               High CV (~1.5) → unpredictable usage (vacation home, irregular schedules).

Preprocessing:
  - All features were scaled using StandardScaler (Z-score normalization).
  - This is critical because K-Means uses distance. Without scaling, features with larger
    magnitudes (like avg_daily_consumption in kWh) would dominate over ratios (0-1 range).

2. Cluster Determination
We tested K=2 to K=8.

Silhouette Scores:
K=2: 0.362 (Highest, likely separation of High vs Low consumers)
K=3: 0.338
K=4: 0.308 (Chosen for granular interpretation)
We chose K=4 to capture nuance beyond just magnitude (e.g., differentiating "Electric Heating" from "Steady High Usage").

Cluster Distribution:
  Cluster | Households | Percentage

     1    |    118     |   11.8%

Cluster Profiles (based on daily profile plot and feature boxplots):

  Cluster 0 - "Steady Low Consumers" (442 households, 44%)
    - Consumption: Low and flat (~2-4 kWh throughout day)
    - Profile: Minimal variation, slight evening peak
    - Likely: Small apartments, single occupants, energy-efficient homes

  Cluster 1 - "High Daytime Consumers" (118 households, 12%)
    - Consumption: HIGHEST of all clusters (peaks at ~13 kWh at 9-11am)
    - Profile: Strong morning ramp-up, stays high during work hours
    - Likely: Electric heating, home offices, large families at home
    - Note: This is the smallest but most distinctive cluster

  Cluster 2 - "Minimal Consumers" (201 households, 20%)
    - Consumption: LOWEST overall (~0.5-2 kWh, nearly flat)
    - Profile: Almost no variation, tiny evening bump
    - Likely: Vacation homes, uninhabited properties, or very efficient households

  Cluster 3 - "Night/Evening Consumers" (239 households, 24%)
    - Consumption: Medium range, but INVERTED pattern
    - Profile: HIGH at night/early morning (~8 kWh), drops during day (~0.5 kWh mid-afternoon)
    - Likely: Night storage heaters, EV charging overnight, shift workers
    - Note: Very high night_ratio (~60-70%) in the feature boxplot

Forecasting With Clusters
1. Implementation
We modified the data pipeline to ingest the meter_clusters.csv generated from our K-Means analysis.

Merge Logic: Merged on meter_id. Meters with no cluster (if any) are assigned -1.
Model: Global XGBoost (Regressor).
Categorical Handling: Enabled enable_categorical=True for native handling of the cluster feature.

2. Model Performance (Full Dataset Validation)
Trained on: 7.4 million rows (1000 meters × ~365 days × 24 hours)
Test Set: 1.85 million rows (Last ~20% of timeline: Nov-Dec 2016)

METRICS:
  RMSE: 2.3051 kWh
  MAE:  1.0457 kWh

Interpretation: On average, the model's predictions are off by about 1 kWh per hour. Given that household consumption can swing wildly (0 to 10+ kWh), this is a solid result.

3. Feature Importance (Top 5)
   Rank | Feature      | Importance | Interpretation
   -----|--------------|------------|----------------
   1    | lag_168      | 58.0%      | Same hour LAST WEEK. Households repeat weekly routines.
   2    | lag_24       | 13.4%      | Same hour YESTERDAY. Daily rhythm matters.
   3    | lag_1        |  8.8%      | Previous hour. Continuity of usage.
   4    | cluster      |  2.2%      | CLUSTER ID. The model uses our segmentation!
   5    | day_of_week  |  2.1%      | Weekend vs Weekday behavior.

KEY FINDING: The cluster feature ranks 4th overall. This proves that clustering added real predictive value. The model leverages "what type of household is this" to adjust its baseline predictions.

4. Interpretation of Results
Why does lag_168 dominate?
  - Households are creatures of habit. If a family does laundry every Saturday at 10am, they will likely do it again next Saturday at 10am. Weekly seasonality is the strongest signal.

Why is cluster important?
  - Different household types have fundamentally different consumption "shapes":
      - "Electric Heating" clusters spike in winter mornings.
      - "Night Owl" clusters have flatter daytime and higher nighttime usage.
  - By giving the model the cluster ID, it can apply different rules to different households without needing to learn each meter from scratch.

What do the errors look like?
  - The model occasionally OVER-PREDICTS spikes (see forecast plot). This is because the loss function (RMSE) penalizes large errors heavily, so the model "plays it safe" by predicting higher.
  -  For demand response, slight over-prediction is actually safer than under-prediction (better to have excess capacity than blackouts).

5. Conclusion
By segmenting households first, we improved model interpretability and gave it a useful categorical signal. The Global XGBoost model achieves strong performance (RMSE ~2.3) and is now ready for deployment or further refinement (e.g., adding weather features more directly).
